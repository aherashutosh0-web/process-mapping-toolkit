# How to Run a Process Mapping Workshop

*Practitioner's notes — the version of this I wish someone had handed me before my first workshop, where I let a room of nine people argue about approval thresholds for two hours and mapped almost nothing.*

---

## Before the workshop

**Get the right people (6–8 max).** You need the people who *do* the process, one or two who *manage* it, and someone who can *decide* when the room disagrees. The doers are non-negotiable — a map built only from managers describes the process as imagined. If you can only get managers, say in the write-up that the map is unvalidated.

**Two sessions beat one long one.** My default: session 1 maps AS-IS (2 hours), session 2 designs TO-BE (2 hours, a week later, same people + the sponsor for the first 20 minutes). Designing the future while documenting the present produces a wishlist.

**Pre-interview 2–3 people.** Fifteen minutes each, phone is fine. You'll learn the vocabulary (you must use *their* words on the map), find the exception paths nobody will mention in a group, and know which two people will disagree so you can seat them where that's productive.

**Bring:** sticky notes (three colours), thick pens (thin pens produce maps only the author can read), a timer, the printed pre-draft from your interviews ("I've sketched something from my calls — destroy it"), and chocolate. Remote? Miro/Mural with the same three-colour discipline, cameras on.

**Set the frame in the invite:** scope in, scope out, the question the map must answer, and the rule that the map documents *today*, not today's complaints. (The complaints are welcome — in the pain-point column, at step level.)

## During the workshop

**Hour 1: get the steps down.**
1. Start at the trigger ("customer clicks 'buy'") and walk the work forward. One sticky per step, verb-first ("*Checks* stock", not "stock check").
2. Colour-code as you go: **yellow = process step, pink = pain point/wait/rework, green = system or data**. The pink notes are your gap analysis seed — protect them.
3. Swimlanes: one lane per *role*. Draw the lanes *after* the notes exist — you'll discover the lane structure from the work, not the org chart.
4. You write the notes if the writing is fiddly, but the steps must come from their mouths. Your job is speed and questions, not content.

**The questions that dig out reality:**
- "And then what *actually* happens?" (the word "actually" does 80% of the work)
- "What happens when that fails / is late / is wrong?"
- "Who waits here? For how long?" (waits are invisible on procedure documents and dominate real lead time)
- "How do you know it's done? What tells you?" (usually reveals a missing system event)
- "Who else touches this between step 4 and 5?" (finds the undocumented handoffs)
- "What did you do the last time this went wrong?" (exception paths)

**Hour 2: verify and measure.**
1. Walk the whole map out loud, trigger to end. Fix arrows, not discussions.
2. Mark every handoff crossing a lane. Count them out loud. ("Seven. That's seven chances to drop the ball — is that fair?") This single number reframes the TO-BE conversation.
3. Put a number on the pain: lead time, volume, error rate, time per order. Even rough ones ("3–5 minutes per order") — estimates beat adjectives when you're prioritising gaps later.
4. Capture the one exception path the process pretends doesn't exist. Ask directly: "what's the messy case?"
5. End with: "what did we *not* map that we should have?" Write the answers down as out-of-scope notes.

**Facilitation traps I've fallen into:**
- **The senior person answers everything.** Redirect constantly: "Fatima, is that how it works on the floor?"
- **Solution talk takes over.** Park it visibly ("that's a TO-BE idea — pink note, on the wall") and move on. Never say "not now" without capturing it.
- **The map grows forever.** Scope creep in a workshop shows up as "and then we also…". Re-point at the scope box.
- **Two people describe two different processes.** Often both are right — variants by site/shift/channel. Map the variants as branches ("if North site…") instead of forcing one flow.

## After the workshop (48 hours, while it's fresh)

1. **Digitise the map** (mermaid, Visio, Miro export — see [process-mapping-notations.md](process-mapping-notations.md)) while the vocabulary is fresh.
2. **Send it back to participants within 2 days** with three asks: "Is this right? What's missing? What's wrong?" A map without a validation round is a rumour.
3. **Turn pink notes into the gap register** ([gap analysis template](../templates/gap-analysis-template.md)) — pain points become gaps, gaps become requirements, requirements get IDs.
4. **Update the process owner** with the handoff count and lead time before anything else. Those two numbers are how the project gets funded.
5. **File the workshop notes** (raw, including the parked solutions) — in six months, someone will ask "why didn't we…?" and the pink note answers it.

## Remote workshops

Same shape, tighter slices: 90 minutes max per session, cameras on, everyone edits the board themselves (driving the mouse for others is where remote workshops go to die), and use voting dots to settle "is this really the biggest pain?". Recruit a second facilitator to watch the chat and queue questions. Expect ~30% less throughput and book accordingly.

## A checklist you can steal

**Before:** scope box written · right 6–8 invited · 2–3 pre-interviews done · pre-draft sketch prepared · materials ready · frame sent in invite

**During:** steps captured verb-first · three-colour discipline held · lanes drawn from the work · handoffs counted · pains numbered · one exception path captured · "what didn't we map?" asked

**After:** digitised ≤48h · validation round done · gaps registered with IDs · owner briefed on the numbers · notes filed

---

*The map is not the deliverable. The deliverable is a room of people who now agree on how the work actually works — the diagram is just how you keep that agreement.*
